### Lesson 1: The SONOFF Product Portfolio: An Installer's Map

SONOFF's catalogue looks large until you organise it by the problem each family solves. Do that once and product selection stops being a browsing exercise.

**The six families, and the job each one owns.**

**DIY smart switches (BASIC, MINI, DUAL).** Small relay modules that sit *behind* an existing switch or inside a fitting. They do not replace the wall plate, which matters more than it sounds: in a property where the client likes the existing switch plates, or where the plates are a non-standard size, a DIY module lets you add control without touching the visible finish. They need space in the back box or the ceiling rose, and that space is the constraint you check first.

**Smart wall switches (Fusion, M5, T5 series).** These replace the wall plate entirely. Cleaner result, single-visit install, and the client gets a physical button that works when the network is down. The constraint here is the back box: most need 5–7 cm depth, and a 4 cm box is common in older Egyptian construction. Check depth before quoting, not on install day.

**Smart plugs (S26, S60).** Appliance-level control with no wiring at all. The quickest win on any site and the right answer for anything the client might move — fans, heaters, water dispensers, washing machines. Rated current is the whole story: check the appliance's inrush, not its running draw.

**Gateways and sensors (ZBBridge, ZBDongle, motion/contact/temperature).** The gateway is what turns a collection of devices into a system. Zigbee sensors are cheap, battery-powered, and only useful once a gateway and enough mains-powered routers exist to carry the mesh.

**Appliance controllers (TH series, NSPanel, DUALR3).** These control something with logic rather than just switching it — a thermostat, a water heater, a motorised curtain. This is where the margin is, because the job is engineering rather than fitting.

**Cameras (CAM series).** A separate conversation with separate privacy implications, covered in a later module.

**The one decision that determines everything else: retrofit or renovation?**

If the walls are staying closed and the client wants minimal disruption, you are in DIY-module and smart-plug territory, and the limiting factors are back-box space and neutral availability. If the property is being renovated or newly wired, you specify wall switches and pull neutrals everywhere, and almost every constraint disappears.

Answering this one question before opening a catalogue removes about eighty percent of the product range from consideration and makes the rest obvious.

**The mistake that costs money.** Quoting from the catalogue instead of from the site. Two properties with identical room counts can need entirely different hardware because one has neutrals at the switch positions and the other does not. The portfolio is not a shopping list; it is a set of answers to questions you have to ask on site first.

### Lesson 2: Communication Protocols: Wi-Fi, Zigbee 3.0 & Matter

Three protocol families appear in SONOFF deployments, and choosing between them is the most consequential technical decision on a job. Get it wrong and you produce an installation that works on commissioning day and degrades for years.

**Wi-Fi: simple, and it does not scale.**

Every Wi-Fi device joins the home router directly. Setup is trivial, no hub is needed, and range is whatever the router covers. That simplicity is real and it is why Wi-Fi devices sell.

The problems appear with count. Consumer routers commonly start misbehaving somewhere around 30 or so associated clients — not by failing outright, but by dropping and re-associating devices unpredictably. A household already has phones, laptops, a TV and a console on that router before you add anything. Twenty smart devices on top is where "it randomly goes offline" starts, and that call comes to you, not to the router manufacturer.

Wi-Fi devices are also mains-powered by necessity: the radio draws far too much for a battery sensor to be practical.

**Use Wi-Fi for:** small installs, a handful of devices, anything where adding a hub is disproportionate, and devices that need internet access anyway.

**Zigbee 3.0: the answer for scale.**

Zigbee devices do not touch the router. They form their own mesh through a gateway. Three consequences matter:

- **Low power.** A Zigbee contact sensor runs a year or more on a coin cell. Wi-Fi cannot do this.
- **Mesh routing.** Every mains-powered Zigbee device acts as a router for the others. Coverage grows as you add devices, rather than degrading.
- **Local operation.** Automations between Zigbee devices run at the gateway, so they keep working when the internet is down. A motion-triggered light that depends on a cloud round trip is a light with a delay and a failure mode.

The cost is the gateway — one more box, one more thing to place well and power reliably.

**Use Zigbee for:** anything above roughly a dozen devices, all battery sensors, and any automation that must survive an internet outage.

**Matter: the compatibility layer, not a competitor.**

Matter is a standard that lets devices from different manufacturers work in the same ecosystem without vendor bridges. A Matter device can join Apple Home, Google Home and Alexa at once. It runs over Wi-Fi or over Thread — it is not itself a radio.

For an installer the practical value is client-facing: Matter is what lets you promise a client that a device bought today will still work if they change ecosystems in three years. That promise closes deals.

**The rule that prevents most callbacks.**

Count the devices before you choose. Under about ten, Wi-Fi is fine and a gateway is overhead. Above that, Zigbee with a gateway is not a preference — it is what stops the installation degrading as it grows. Mixed installs are normal and correct: Wi-Fi for the few devices that need it, Zigbee for everything else.

The failure to avoid is putting forty Wi-Fi devices on a domestic router because each individual device was simple to commission. Every one of them worked the day you installed it. That is precisely why the pattern keeps happening.

### Lesson 3: Working with the eWeLink Platform

eWeLink is where the installation becomes a system the client can actually operate. Treating it as an afterthought is how good hardware work produces a bad handover.

**What the platform does.** Device pairing, grouping into rooms, scene and automation building, scheduling, remote access, firmware updates, and multi-user sharing. For most clients it is the entire product — they will never see the wiring you were proud of.

**Account ownership: decide this before you pair anything.**

This is the single biggest process mistake in the field, and it is almost always discovered too late.

If you pair every device under *your* installer account and hand the client a shared login, you have created a dependency that will hurt both sides. The client cannot fully control their own home, cannot remove you, and if your account has a problem their house is affected. Meanwhile you are permanently responsible for an account tied to a property you no longer visit.

**The correct pattern:**

1. Create the account **in the client's name, with the client's email**, at the start of the job.
2. Commission everything under that account.
3. Have the client **share access back to you** using eWeLink's sharing feature.
4. At handover the client owns the home. You hold shared access for support, and they can revoke it at any time.

This costs five extra minutes at the start and prevents an entire category of dispute later. It also positions you correctly: you are the professional they trust with access, not the gatekeeper holding their house hostage.

**Rooms and naming.** Group devices into rooms that match how the household talks about the property, not how the wiring runs. "Kitchen" is a room; "Circuit 4" is not. Use the client's own vocabulary — if they say صالة, the room is the one they call صالة. A device list that reads like a distribution board is a device list nobody uses.

**Scenes and automations.** eWeLink separates manual scenes (the client taps to run) from automations (triggered by time, device state, or sensor). Both are covered properly in the automation module; what matters here is that you build them *with* the client present, not after they leave. A scene designed in the van is a scene nobody asked for.

**Sharing for support.** Shared users can control devices and see status but cannot delete the account or remove the owner. That is exactly the access level a support relationship needs. Document what you can and cannot see, so the client is not surprised later.

**Local control, and its honest limits.** Some eWeLink functions work on the local network without internet; cloud features and remote access do not. Tell the client plainly which is which. "It works without internet" is only true for part of the system, and a client who discovers the difference during an outage — rather than at handover — loses confidence in everything you installed.

**Firmware.** Updates arrive through the app. Never run a bulk update on a site you are about to leave: if an update misbehaves you want to be standing there. Update, verify, then hand over.

### Lesson 4: Product Selection for Real-World Scenarios

Product selection is a sequence of constraint checks, not a preference. Run them in order and the answer is usually forced — which is exactly what you want, because a forced answer is one you can defend to a client.

**Check 1 — Is there a neutral at the switch position?**

This single question eliminates more product options than everything else combined, and it is the one most often skipped.

Most smart wall switches need line, neutral and load, because the electronics must draw power continuously. Older Egyptian installations frequently have only line and switched-line at the switch box, with the neutral joined at the ceiling rose.

If there is no neutral you have three honest options: a no-neutral-compatible switch (which works by leaking a small current through the load and can cause LED lamps to glow faintly or flicker); a DIY relay module fitted at the ceiling rose where the neutral is; or pulling a neutral, which means chasing walls.

Tell the client which of these applies *before* quoting. A no-neutral switch that makes their new LED downlights glow at night is a callback you cannot fix with configuration.

**Check 2 — Does the back box have depth?**

Smart switches need roughly 5–7 cm. A 4 cm box is common in older construction. You cannot compress the module. Measure during the survey, and if the depth is short, price a deeper back box or plan a DIY module elsewhere in the circuit.

**Check 3 — What is the load type and what is its inrush?**

Resistive loads (heaters, incandescent) are well-behaved. Inductive loads (motors, fans, pumps) draw a starting surge. LED drivers draw a very brief but very high inrush as their input capacitors charge — a bank of LED downlights can peak at many times its running current.

Match the device to the *peak*, not to the nameplate. A relay rated 10 A that runs a 2 A LED bank looks generous and still welds its contacts if the inrush is high enough and it switches often enough.

Dimmers add a further constraint: the lamp must be dimmable and the dimmer must suit its driver type. "Dimmable LED" on a box is not a guarantee of compatibility with a given dimmer.

**Check 4 — Does the client move this appliance?**

If yes, it is a smart plug, regardless of what else would be elegant. Hard-wiring control to a socket the client will unplug something from produces a device that controls nothing.

**Check 5 — Does this need to work when the internet is down?**

Anything safety-adjacent, anything on a stair or entrance, and anything the client relies on daily should keep working offline. That pushes you toward Zigbee with local automation, and away from cloud-dependent Wi-Fi logic.

**Check 6 — How many devices in total?**

Under ten, Wi-Fi is acceptable. Above that, specify a gateway and Zigbee, as covered in the protocol lesson.

**A worked example.** Three-bedroom flat, walls staying closed, client wants lighting control throughout plus the water heater and the AC.

Survey finds: neutrals present in the two newest rooms only, 4 cm boxes in the hallway, water heater on its own 20 A circuit, split AC with an IR remote.

The forced answer: wall switches in the two rooms with neutrals; DIY modules at the ceiling roses elsewhere; a deeper back box quoted for the hallway or a module instead; a high-current-rated switch or contactor arrangement for the water heater — never a standard 10 A relay; an IR bridge for the AC, because the AC's own controller stays in charge and you are only replacing the remote.

Notice that almost none of that came from the catalogue. It came from six questions asked on site.
